On a dropshipping model, most of this changes

The two picks below were built on private-label economics. Three of the four inputs they rest on do not survive the switch.

A $35 dropship product can afford a CPC of $0.12–0.20. Real CPCs run $0.50–1.50. To afford a $0.80 CPC at 1% conversion you would need to sell at roughly $208. This is the arithmetic behind the statistic that 80–90% of new dropshippers fail within a year — the product selection is rarely what kills them.

What this leaves.
Exactly one viable configuration: organic-first acquisition, where content costs time rather than cost-per-click, with paid traffic switched on only after a creative is already proven to convert. That flips the product criteria entirely — the winner is whatever is most watchable, not whatever has the best category benchmark.

Alternative theme — and the better one

No-heat self-care tools

Hair and face tools, sold as overnight ritual kits. Runs the same screen and beats the pet bundle on every economic measure.

Two findings move this ahead of pet. First, beauty tools and devices carry a 24% best-seller rate — close to one in four becomes a best seller, which is an unusually forgiving base rate for a category where product selection is normally the gamble. Second, supplier costs of $1–10 against $15–50 retail produce the highest margins available anywhere in dropshipping: gua sha and jade roller sets are quoted at 90–95% gross before shipping.

Critically, tools are not cosmetics. Nothing here is applied to the body as a substance, so MoCRA never attaches — unlike the eyebrow and eyeliner stamp kits that appear on the same trend lists and quietly carry pigment, and with it the whole cosmetics regime.

The claims line still applies, without the paperwork.
Tools escape MoCRA, but marketing can drag them back under drug rules. "Depuffs", "sculpts the appearance of", "reduces friction and breakage" are safe. "Detoxifies lymph", "removes wrinkles", "prevents hair loss", "treats" are drug claims and invite the same warning letters the serum category attracts. And do not extend the line into eyebrow or eyeliner stamps — the moment pigment is involved, it is a cosmetic.

Runner-up: the rotating snack tray (#15) — a stronger pure-virality hook, but a one-time novelty with no bundle path and a decaying trend window. The pet bundle has repeat customers across accessories even without a consumable.

On ceiling, honestly.
Dropshipping is a cash-flow business, not an equity one. There is no dropship path to the $3.5B outcome cited below — you do not own the product, the formula or the supply. The realistic ambition is a store that generates cash on low capital, and which can fund a private-label transition later. The pet route is chosen partly because that transition is available: proven accessory buyers are exactly who you would later sell an own-brand product to.

Private-label scenario — if only one product gets funded

Functional dog dental chews

Product #6 — and deliberately not the highest-converting product on this list.

Why not snail mucin, despite the better benchmark. That shelf is already locked. On the core keyword the top three listings take 79.7% of clicks and 72.3% of conversions, and the top five take 93% of clicks. A new brand there pays for the traffic and hands the conversion to Cosrx. High demand, low chance of capturing any of it.

Dental chews win on probability rather than ceiling. The category's online shelf is still being built — which is the whole opportunity.

Channel maturity~18% DTC today → 30–35% by 2035
Channel growth~17.2% CAGR online + subscription
Subscription proof+19% auto-ship in one year
Reorder cycle~30 days — CAC spreads over 6–12 orders
Category conversion4.40% — third highest here
Gross margin60–70%, survives paid traffic
ReturnsNear zero — no sizing, no fit risk
Problem visibilityOwner notices the breath daily

The wedge. Greenies owns retail, but it is dental-only and carries a persistent ingredient-quality objection among exactly the buyer who shops direct. Premiumization is moving toward functional chews — dental plus a second benefit: breath, probiotic, joint. Jinx, Red Barn and The Missing Link already prove a challenger can land. Position as a clean-label functional chew sold subscription-first, not as a cheaper Greenies.

The cost of entry is the moat. Dental chews are regulated as pet food, not as a generic chew: treats require licensing, AAFCO ingredient and quantity statements, "treat" on the principal display panel, state feed registration, and an FDA-registered manufacturing facility. That is real work — which is precisely why casual dropshippers cannot flood this the way they flood beauty gadgets.

The one condition that flips this answer.
It needs private-label inventory and label compliance before first revenue. If the store has to launch dropship-only with no inventory capital, take #2 peptide lip treatment instead — same anchor category, impulse price, no compliance burden — and run it as the cash-generation step that funds this one.

Private-label scenario — if no license is acceptable

Peptide + rosemary scalp serum

Product #8 — the only license-free option that carries the top conversion rate and a real ceiling.

A no-licensing constraint rules out the dental chews (pet treats must be licensed) and every supplement on the list. It does not rule out topical skincare — which reopens the highest-converting category available.

Licensing status. Cosmetics need no license and no FDA pre-approval. Under MoCRA the brand owner is the "responsible person," but the small-business exemption applies below $1M average annual gross sales, covering facility registration and product listing. That exemption specifically excludes products contacting mucous membranes — which is why a scalp serum qualifies and the lip treatment (#2) and eyeliner (#11) do not.

Roughly the same number of competing listings, but demand spread across about 2.8× more of them. Supporting signals agree: weekly search volume on "scalp serum" runs ~7,768 and rising (+3.24% week over week), the leading listing cleared 70,000 orders in a month, and the fastest-growing listing is up 33.3% month over month — new entrants are actively taking share, which does not happen on a closed shelf. The brand mix is fragmented (Nutrafol, The Ordinary, Divi, K18, Vegamour, Aromatica, Botanic Hearth) with no dominator of the Cosrx or Greenies kind.

Caveats: Amazon concentration is a proxy for demand structure, not a direct read on a Shopify storefront's odds. And "scalp serum" is the head term at ~7,768 weekly searches while "hair scalp serum" draws only ~319 — build the keyword strategy on the former.

Why it beats the other license-free options. Beauty tools (#12 heatless curls) are equally free of licensing but are commodity accessories with no repeat purchase. Personalized apparel (#20) caps out near 40% margin. Home gadgets sit in a 2–3% conversion category. None of them has a $3.5B outcome in living memory; this category does.

Paid-search economics — the real constraint. Beauty & personal care Google Ads CPC averages $5.70 in 2026 (range $4.28–$7.70), against a $5.42 all-industry average. Divided by the 5.37% category conversion rate:

At $35 retail, 75% margin less ~$7 shipping and fees, first-order contribution is about $19.

Retention is what decides this, and it is unforgiving. Beauty and skincare repeat-purchase rates run 30–45% (serums at the high end), 60–70% of subscribers are lost between order one and order three, and 44% of cancellations land inside the first 90 days. A 40% first-repeat rate improving to ~75% thereafter yields ~2.1 expected orders per customer — not the five or six a naive payback calculation assumes. That single input decides the business:

Only path C works, and even it sits below the 3:1 LTV/CAC that DTC investors treat as healthy. The product is fine and the shelf is open — acquiring customers on paid search at benchmark CPC is what fails.

The $50 content-led CAC is an assumption rather than a measurement: if content acquisition does not work, CAC stays near $106 and no viable path exists.

The single rule that keeps it license-free.
Claims determine classification, not ingredients. Saying the product grows hair, regrows, restores growth or prevents hair loss makes it a drug under FDA law — the same category as minoxidil — and FDA has issued warning letters to cosmetic firms for exactly this. Stay in appearance language: thicker-looking, fuller appearance, reduces breakage, strengthens, supports scalp health. Every ad, influencer brief and landing page has to hold that line — one creator saying "it regrew my hair" is the real exposure, not the label.

Nutrafol's headline product is an ingestible, which is a supplement and does carry the manufacturing burden. The topical serum is the license-free route to the same customer.

The constraint that shaped the list

Demand alone is a trap. Jewelry, electronics and baby gear all draw enormous traffic and convert near the floor — they are high-consideration, high-AOV or high-return. The same visitors, pointed at a consumable beauty or pet product, convert roughly five times more often. That spread is the single biggest lever available before a store has any traffic history.

Don't launch all twenty

Twenty SKUs splits the ad budget twenty ways, and none of them accumulate enough data to optimize. Launch eight — two clean customer avatars with a bundle path inside each — prove the funnel, then widen from the list above.

Handle before launch

Supplements — #3, #5, #10, and pet #7. Structure/function claims only, no disease claims. Use a cGMP-compliant manufacturer and keep certificates of analysis on file. This is the highest-liability group on the list.

Red-light mask — #18. Clearance has to come from the supplier's device, not from a claim you write. Ask for the documentation before the first PO.

Beauty consumables. Batch and expiry tracking, plus cosmetic ingredient labelling for every destination market you ship to.

Electronics — #19. Highest return rate here; budget 8–12% returns into the margin rather than discovering it in month three.

Tier 2 decays. Virality is a window, not a moat. Tier 1 is the durable business — keep the catalogue's centre of gravity there.
